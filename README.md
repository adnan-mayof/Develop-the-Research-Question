## 👩‍🔬 Story 2 — “Maya Narrows It Down”

Maya has finished comparing the previous reviews.

She returns to her mentor.

**Maya:** “I think I found a gap. But now I need to turn it into my own research question.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. What do you want to know?”

**Maya:** “I want to know if AI-powered learning technologies improve student learning.”

Her mentor nods.

**Mentor:** “That's a good starting point, but it's still too broad. Let's make it more specific.”

**Maya:** “What should I change?”

**Mentor:** “First, tell me **who** you're interested in.”

**Maya:** “Students.”

**Mentor:** “Which students?”

**Maya:** “Students in higher education.”

**Mentor:** “Good. Now, what exactly are you studying?”

**Maya:** “AI-powered learning technologies.”

**Mentor:** “And what are you comparing them with?”

**Maya:** “Traditional instruction or a control condition.”

**Mentor:** “Good. Finally, what do you mean by ‘student learning’?”

**Maya:** “Learning outcomes—things like academic achievement, knowledge, or performance.”

Maya writes everything down.

**Maya:** “So instead of asking, ‘Does AI improve learning?’ I need to specify who, what AI intervention, what comparison, and what outcome.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. Those elements will help you turn your broad idea into a focused research question.”

Maya looks at her notes.

**Maya:** “Is there a specific way I should organize all of these elements?”

**Mentor:** “Yes. Researchers often use **question frameworks** to structure their research questions. But there is something important you need to understand first.”

**Maya:** “What's that?”

**Mentor:** “Not every research question is the same. If you are asking whether an intervention produces an effect, you may use one framework. If you are studying an exposure, people's experiences, or mapping an area of evidence, you may need a different framework.”

**Maya:** “So I shouldn't automatically use PICO?”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. **Choose the framework based on the type of question you are asking and the type of evidence you plan to synthesize.**”

Maya nods.

**Maya:** “Then I need to understand the different frameworks before I decide which one fits my question.”

**Mentor:** “That's the next step.”

---

# 1. Why Use a Question Framework?

Once the research gap has been identified, formulate a **focused research question**.

> **PRISMA (2020), Item 4 — Objectives:** State the review objectives and research questions explicitly.

A question framework helps the researcher organize the important elements of the research question.

Depending on the review, these elements may include:

* **Population** — Who is being studied?
* **Intervention** — What intervention is being evaluated?
* **Exposure** — What factor or condition is being examined?
* **Comparison** — What is the intervention or exposure being compared with?
* **Outcome** — What is being measured?
* **Study design** — What types of studies are eligible?
* **Time** — Is timing or intervention duration important?
* **Context** — Where or under what circumstances does the phenomenon occur?
* **Experience or phenomenon of interest** — What experiences or phenomena are being examined?

The important point is that **different research questions require different structures**.

> **Do not force every research question into PICO.**

---

# 2. PICO

Maya's question is asking whether an **intervention produces an effect**. This makes PICO a natural framework to consider.

Use **PICO** primarily for intervention questions where the goal is to determine whether an intervention produces an effect.

| Component            | Meaning                                       |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| **P — Population**   | Who is being studied?                         |
| **I — Intervention** | What intervention is being evaluated?         |
| **C — Comparison**   | What is the intervention being compared with? |
| **O — Outcome**      | What outcome is being measured?               |

### Example PICO Question

> **Among university students (P), what is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies (I), compared with traditional instruction or a control condition (C), on student learning outcomes (O)?**

Maya looks at the question.

**Maya:** “That looks very similar to the question I was developing.”

**Mentor:** “Yes. PICO helps you make sure that the major elements of your intervention question are clearly defined.”

But Maya has another question.

**Maya:** “What if the study design matters too?”

**Mentor:** “Then you can extend PICO.”

---

# 3. PICOS

Use **PICOS** when **study design is an important part of the eligibility criteria**.

| Component            | Meaning                               |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| **P — Population**   | Who is being studied?                 |
| **I — Intervention** | What intervention is being evaluated? |
| **C — Comparison**   | What is the comparator?               |
| **O — Outcome**      | What outcomes are measured?           |
| **S — Study Design** | What study designs are eligible?      |

### Example PICOS Question

> **Among university students (P), what is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies (I), compared with traditional instruction or a control condition (C), on student learning outcomes (O), based on experimental and quasi-experimental studies (S)?**

**Maya:** “So PICOS is useful when I also need to define the types of studies I will include.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

---

# 4. PICOT

**Maya:** “What if the length of the intervention is important?”

**Mentor:** “Then you may want to include time.”

Use **PICOT** when the **timing or duration** of an intervention is important.

| Component            | Meaning      |
| -------------------- | ------------ |
| **P — Population**   | Population   |
| **I — Intervention** | Intervention |
| **C — Comparison**   | Comparator   |
| **O — Outcome**      | Outcome      |
| **T — Time**         | Time period  |

### Example PICOT Question

> **Among university students (P), what is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies (I), compared with traditional instruction (C), on learning outcomes (O) following interventions lasting at least four weeks (T)?**

**Maya:** “So I don't add T just because it exists. I add it when time is important to my question.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

---

# 5. PECO

Maya begins to wonder whether every research question involves an intervention.

**Maya:** “What if I'm not studying an intervention? What if I'm studying whether something is associated with an outcome?”

**Mentor:** “Then PICO may not be the best fit. You might consider PECO.”

Use **PECO** when the question concerns an **exposure, factor, or condition** rather than an intervention.

| Component          | Meaning            |
| ------------------ | ------------------ |
| **P — Population** | Population         |
| **E — Exposure**   | Exposure or factor |
| **C — Comparison** | Comparison group   |
| **O — Outcome**    | Outcome            |

### Example PECO Question

> **Among university students (P), is exposure to generative AI use in learning environments (E), compared with little or no generative AI use (C), associated with differences in academic learning outcomes (O)?**

**Maya:** “So the difference is that I'm not necessarily assigning an intervention. I'm examining an exposure or factor.”

**Mentor:** “Correct.”

---

# 6. SPIDER

Maya thinks about another possibility.

**Maya:** “What if I wanted to understand how students experience AI instead of measuring whether AI improves their test scores?”

**Mentor:** “That's a different kind of question. For experience-focused research, you might use SPIDER.”

Use **SPIDER** when the review includes **qualitative, mixed-methods, or experience-focused research**.

| Component                       | Meaning                                     |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **S — Sample**                  | Participants                                |
| **PI — Phenomenon of Interest** | Experience or phenomenon                    |
| **D — Design**                  | Research design                             |
| **E — Evaluation**              | Perceptions, experiences, attitudes, etc.   |
| **R — Research Type**           | Qualitative, quantitative, or mixed methods |

### Example SPIDER Question

> **How do university students (S) experience the use of generative AI for learning (PI), as examined through qualitative and mixed-methods research designs (D), particularly regarding their perceptions of usefulness, engagement, and learning (E), across qualitative and mixed-methods studies (R)?**

**Maya:** “That's very different from my intervention question.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. The framework changes because the research question changes.”

---

# 7. PCC

**Maya:** “What if I don't want to estimate an effect at all? What if I want to understand what evidence exists across a broad topic?”

**Mentor:** “Then you may be thinking about a **scoping review**.”

Use **PCC** when conducting a **scoping review**, particularly when the objective is to map the breadth and characteristics of evidence rather than estimate an intervention effect.

| Component          | Meaning         |
| ------------------ | --------------- |
| **P — Population** | Population      |
| **C — Concept**    | Central concept |
| **C — Context**    | Context         |

### Example PCC Question

> **What evidence exists on the use of artificial intelligence for student learning (Concept) among university students (Population) across higher-education learning environments (Context)?**

**Maya:** “So PCC helps me ask what evidence exists and how that evidence is distributed across a topic.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

---

# 8. PICo

Maya has one more question.

**Maya:** “And what if I want to study students' experiences within a particular setting?”

**Mentor:** “Then PICo may be useful.”

Use **PICo** for qualitative questions, particularly when examining **experiences, perceptions, or phenomena**.

| Component          | Meaning                |
| ------------------ | ---------------------- |
| **P — Population** | Population             |
| **I — Interest**   | Phenomenon of interest |
| **Co — Context**   | Context                |

### Example PICo Question

> **How do university students (P) experience the use of generative AI for learning (I) within higher-education learning environments (Co)?**

---

# 9. Choosing the Appropriate Framework

Maya now has a clearer picture.

**Maya:** “I see it now. The framework depends on what I am actually trying to find out.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly. Start with your research question—not with the framework.”

Use the **research gap and the type of question** to determine which framework fits.

```text
What type of question are you asking?
              ↓
      ┌───────┴────────┐
      ↓                ↓
 Intervention?       Exposure?
      ↓                ↓
    PICO              PECO
      │
      ├── Study design important? → PICOS
      │
      └── Time important? → PICOT

Experience / Qualitative?
      ↓
 PICo / SPIDER

Scoping / Mapping Evidence?
      ↓
 PCC
```

---

# 10. An Important Principle

Maya reviews her notes one more time.

**Maya:** “I think I understand something important now. The framework isn't the research question itself.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

The framework is a **tool for structuring the question**, not a requirement to use every component in the final wording.

A researcher can use a framework to identify the important elements of a question and then write the final question in clear, natural language.

For example:

> **What is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies on student learning outcomes, and which characteristics of the intervention, learners, learning context, and study methodology explain variation in these effects?**

This question does not need to mechanically list every component as separate phrases. The framework helped structure the thinking behind the question.

---

# 11. Maya Formulates Her Research Question

Maya returns to her original question.

**Maya:** “So my original question was too broad.”

**Mentor:** “Yes.”

**Maya:** “I narrowed it by identifying the population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes. Then I considered whether study design or time needed to be part of the question.”

**Mentor:** “Exactly.”

Maya writes her question:

> **What is the effect of AI-powered learning technologies on student learning outcomes, and which characteristics of the intervention, learners, learning context, and study methodology explain variation in these effects?**

She looks at her mentor.

**Maya:** “Now I have a research question that I can actually build my systematic review around.”

**Mentor:** “Good. Your question will now guide the next decisions in your review—including what evidence you will consider eligible.”

Maya closes her notebook.

She is ready for the next step.
